## Do I need a breast lift?

You may, if your nipple sits below the crease under your breast when you're not wearing a bra, or points down, and that bothers you. [Umbareen Mahmood, MD, writing on the ASPS website](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/blog/to-breast-lift-or-not-to-breast-lift), says "any degree of nipple drooping below the fold may indicate that a breast lift is needed." No home test can settle it, and the choice is yours: [ASPS](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/breast-lift/candidates) says to do it for yourself, not "to try to fit any sort of ideal image."

A lift raises the breast by removing extra skin and tightening the tissue around it, and [ASPS](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/breast-lift) says it "does not significantly change the size of your breasts or round out the upper part of your breast." So there are really two questions: has the breast dropped, and do you also want a different size? [The breast lift page explains what a lift changes and what it leaves alone](/procedures/breast-lift-miami#what-is-a-breast-lift).

## How can I tell at home if I need a breast lift?

Compare your nipple with the crease under your breast, in a mirror, without a bra. It's a rough look at the same thing surgeons start from, not a measurement.

1. **Stand side-on to a mirror.** Shoulders relaxed, arms at your sides.
2. **Find the crease.** It's where the underside of your breast meets your chest wall. You may need to feel for it with your fingers.
3. **Compare the levels.** Is your nipple above the crease, level with it or below it?
4. **Check the direction.** Does your nipple point forward or down?
5. **Look at the tissue.** Does most of the breast hang below the crease, even if the nipple doesn't?
6. **Repeat on the other side.** One breast may sit lower than the other.

In a [2015 paper in the Journal of Evaluation in Clinical Practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5055840/), Lee and colleagues describe the fold as "usually hidden behind the breast mound" and say judging ptosis by eye "has low inter- and intra-observer agreement." So two observers, or one observer looking twice, can land on different grades.

## What is the pencil test for breasts, and does it work?

The pencil test is an informal home check: you place a pencil in the crease under your breast and see whether it stays there. It can prompt a useful question, but it can't tell you whether you need a lift. Two surgeons writing on the ASPS website describe it differently, and we found no study that tested it.

| | [Umbareen Mahmood, MD, 2019](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/blog/to-breast-lift-or-not-to-breast-lift) | [Rod Rohrich, MD, FACS, 2017](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/blog/breast-augmentation-vs-breast-lift-picking-the-right-procedure-for-you) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the pencil goes | At the breast crease | Placed "as high as you can under the breast fold" |
| If the pencil stays | The breasts "may have drooped enough to need a breast lift" | You "have signs of breast drooping" |
| What else to check | Nothing further | Whether your nipple falls below the pencil, which he calls "significant ptosis" |
| How they rate the test | Calls it "a helpful trick" but "not entirely precise, and not exactly the same approach" surgeons use | Calls it "a simple test" |

What the test can't see:

- **Why the pencil stayed.** As we read it, it may only show that some breast tissue sits over the crease, which could also be true when the nipple is above the crease, or with a fuller breast.
- **Your skin.** [The Aesthetic Society](https://www.theaestheticsociety.org/procedures/breast/breast-lift/detailed-procedure-information) says the technique depends on "your breast size and shape, degree of breast sagging, skin quality, skin elasticity, and how much extra skin you have."
- **What you want.** A pencil can't weigh whether the change bothers you.

If you try it, the more useful part is where your nipple sits against the pencil, because surgeons grade sagging by nipple position.

## Why do breasts sag?

Breasts sag as skin and supporting tissue stretch and lose elasticity. [Cleveland Clinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/treatments/23298-breast-lift) lists aging, breastfeeding, genetics, gravity, pregnancy and significant weight loss as causes.

Sources differ on breastfeeding. In a study of 132 patients, the number of pregnancies and significant weight loss were linked to sagging, but breastfeeding was not ([Rinker and colleagues, Annals of Plastic Surgery, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20354434/)). Age, higher body mass index, larger cup size and a history of smoking were also risk factors in that study, which came from one center.

## What are the breast ptosis grades?

Ptosis is the medical word for sagging, and the usual scale is the Regnault classification, which grades it by where the nipple sits against the crease. [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK567792/), a medical reference on the NCBI Bookshelf, sets it out like this:

| Grade | Where the nipple sits |
| --- | --- |
| Grade 1, mild | Level with the crease |
| Grade 2, moderate | Below the crease, not the lowest point |
| Grade 3, severe | Below the crease, at the lowest point |
| Pseudoptosis | At or above the crease, tissue below |

![A four-panel diagram of breast ptosis grades, drawn as a thin contour line, a dashed gold crease line and a gold dot. Grade 1, mild: level with the crease. Grade 2, moderate: below the crease, not the lowest point. Grade 3, severe: below the crease, at the lowest point. Pseudoptosis: at or above the crease, tissue below.](IMAGE:grades)

Pseudoptosis means the nipple sits high enough but most of the breast has settled below the crease. Dr. Mahmood says these patients "may still be candidates for a breast lift."

StatPearls grades 1 to 3 by position, not centimeters, though it adds that in pseudoptosis the nipple-to-crease distance "is often greater than 6 cm." So be wary of a centimeter cut-off for a grade that you see online. And the names aren't fully settled: [Davison and Spear, in Seminars in Plastic Surgery](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884715/), separate "glandular ptosis" from pseudoptosis while saying the two seem "virtually the same", and they call the scale "not significantly comprehensive" because it focuses mainly on nipple position.

## What does each grade mean for my options?

More sagging generally points to more incision, and more incision means more scar. [The Aesthetic Society](https://www.theaestheticsociety.org/procedures/breast/breast-lift/detailed-procedure-information) says "the more incisions the surgeon makes, the greater the ability the surgeon has to modify the breast." It uses the words mild, moderate and severe without tying them to grade numbers, so read this as a rough pairing:

| Sagging, in the Society's words | Incision it links | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minor, or a lift with augmentation | Crescent | Less common, with less lift than other incisions |
| Mild, or uneven areolas | Donut | Produces "the least amount of scarring" |
| Moderate, with no implants wanted | Lollipop, or keyhole | Produces "moderate scarring" |
| Severe, or a lift with a reduction | Anchor | Produces "the greatest lift, but also leaves the most scars" |

Surgeons don't all follow that pairing. StatPearls says vertical mastopexy, the lollipop pattern, "is indicated for any degree of ptosis." Our guide to [the types of breast lift](/blog/types-of-breast-lift) explains each incision, and you can [see before and after photos in our gallery](/gallery/breast-lift).

## Do I need a lift, implants or a reduction?

It depends on whether you want to change the breast's position, its size, or both. The Aesthetic Society says that if you're "happy with your breast size, but not the way your breasts look, you may benefit from a breast lift." ASPS says to consider adding [augmentation](/procedures/breast-augmentation-miami) if you want your breasts to look fuller, and adding a [reduction](/procedures/breast-reduction-miami) if you want them smaller.

| What you notice | What surgeons often discuss | Read next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breasts have dropped and you like the size | A lift alone | [A lift without implants](/blog/breast-lift-without-implants) |
| Breasts have dropped and you want more fullness | A lift with implants | [A lift with implants](/blog/breast-lift-implants-miami) |
| Position looks fine and you only want more size | Implants alone may be enough | [Augmentation or a lift](/blog/breast-augmentation-vs-breast-lift) |
| Breasts feel heavy and you want them smaller | A reduction, which also reshapes and raises the breast | [A lift or a reduction](/blog/breast-lift-vs-reduction-miami) |

Implants alone have a limit. [Lawrence Iteld, MD, writing on the ASPS website](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/blog/what-are-the-benefits-of-combining-breast-augmentation-and-a-breast-lift), says "augmentation on its own isn't enough to address sagging breasts." A lift on heavy breasts has one too: [The Aesthetic Society](https://www.theaestheticsociety.org/procedures/breast/breast-lift) says "the results may not be as long-lasting." [ASPS](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/reconstructive-procedures/breast-reduction) describes a reduction as removing skin and tissue, "followed by reshaping and elevating."

## Am I a candidate for a breast lift?

You may be, according to [ASPS](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/breast-lift/candidates), if you're physically healthy, keep a stable weight, don't smoke and are bothered by sagging or lost shape. These eight questions, mostly from its list, can help you sort your thoughts. There's no score, and this isn't a diagnosis.

1. Without a bra, do your nipples fall below the breast crease?
2. Do your nipples and areolas point downward?
3. Have your breasts become flatter or longer, with stretched skin or larger areolas?
4. Is one breast lower than the other?
5. Does this bother you, for your own reasons?
6. Are you physically healthy, with a stable weight?
7. Do you not smoke, or are you ready to stop?
8. Have you finished having children and breastfeeding?

The first five are about your breasts, and yes answers make the topic worth raising with a surgeon. The last three are about whether now is the time. [What surgeons look for in a candidate](/procedures/breast-lift-miami#candidate) goes further, and your surgeon decides at consultation, after an exam.

## Who should wait before having a breast lift?

Anyone pregnant or breastfeeding, planning another pregnancy, still losing weight or smoking should usually wait, and so should anyone with a health problem to treat first.

- **Pregnant or breastfeeding.** [StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK567792/) says to postpone surgery. [Shahram Salemy, MD, writing on the ASPS website](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/news/blog/better-breasts-after-pregnancy-and-breastfeeding), suggests waiting up to 6 months after breastfeeding stops before breast surgery. See [a breast lift after pregnancy](/blog/breast-lift-miami-post-pregnancy).
- **Planning more children.** [ASPS](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/breast-lift/results) says changes in pregnancy "can minimize or reverse the improvement a breast lift provides."
- **Still losing weight.** [Toomey and Cape, writing in StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK610682/), say to delay until you're near your goal weight or at a stable weight. See [a breast lift after weight loss or Ozempic](/blog/breast-lift-recovery-weight-loss-miami).
- **Smoking.** Not smoking is on the ASPS candidate list, and [its preparation page](https://www.plasticsurgery.org/cosmetic-procedures/breast-lift/preparation) says to stop.
- **A health problem to treat first.** Toomey and Cape list active breast cancer, active breast infection and not being medically stable for anesthesia as reasons not to have a lift, and say other conditions should be brought under control first.

If several of these questions point to yes, the next step is an exam, not another test. [Book a consultation](/free-consultation) with Dr. Victoria Karlinsky, MD, FACS, at Alluring in Miami, or start with a [virtual consultation](/fly-in-consultation) if you live in another state.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is breast ptosis?

Breast ptosis is the medical term for sagging. Umbareen Mahmood, MD, writing on the ASPS website, explains that breast tissue loses elasticity and the breast begins to droop, and that the degree is classified by the position of the nipple. Whether to do anything about it is your decision, made with a surgeon.

### Can you fix sagging breasts without surgery?

Not the way a lift does, because a lift removes skin and raises the nipple. The Aesthetic Society says options with minimal incisions may suit very little sagging, but "you will not have the same amount of lift" and the results don't last as long. See our guide to the [non-surgical breast lift](/blog/non-surgical-breast-lift).

### Do small breasts ever need a lift?

Yes. Sagging goes by where the nipple and tissue sit, not by cup size, and StatPearls notes that ptosis can follow a drop in breast volume, such as after significant weight loss. A lift alone won't add fullness, so if you also want more size, ask your surgeon whether [implants would go with it](/blog/breast-lift-implants-miami).

### What if one breast sags more than the other?

That's one of the signs on the ASPS candidate list, which says "One breast is lower than the other." At your exam, the surgeon may measure from the notch at the base of your neck to each nipple, a distance StatPearls says identifies asymmetry between the two. Ask how even your breasts can realistically look afterward.

### Is there a BMI limit for a breast lift?

The ASPS candidate list gives no BMI number. It asks that you're physically healthy and keep a stable weight. In the Rinker study, higher body mass index was linked to sagging, but that's a risk factor, not a cut-off. Your surgeon looks at your overall health and decides at consultation.

### Am I too young or too old for a breast lift?

No source we read sets an age. The ASPS candidate list has no age in it, and looks instead at your health, weight, smoking and the changes in your breasts. Timing around pregnancy and breastfeeding usually matters more, and our [guide to timing a lift after pregnancy](/blog/breast-lift-miami-post-pregnancy) covers that.
