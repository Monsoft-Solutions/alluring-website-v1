# Audience psychology

Who reads a procedure page, what she's afraid of, what she wants, and how
the page earns the click on "Book". Use this to write the strategy brief and
to judge every section: **does it answer the question she has at that point
in the scroll, and does it make the next step feel safe?**

Treat the per-procedure notes at the end as hypotheses. Confirm them against
this page's Search Console queries, the reviews that name the procedure and
the competitor sweep before writing copy.

## Who she is

- A woman aged 25–55, in South Florida or flying in from another US state.
  The practice serves the US only and does not coordinate travel.
- On a phone (60%+ of visits), often in short sessions over days or weeks.
  The purchase is high-consideration and research-heavy: she compares
  surgeons, prices, results and horror stories before she calls anyone.
- Values quality and wants it within reach. The brand promise is "Luxury
  Surgeries Made Affordable". The page proves that with specifics, not by
  repeating the words.
- Many speak Spanish at home. The page is English, but "Hablamos español"
  is a real reassurance, and reviews in Spanish exist.
- She arrives from Google (often a cost or "near me" query), from an AI
  answer that cited a number, from Instagram, or from a paid ad. She may
  already know what the procedure is. She doesn't yet trust this clinic.

## The questions she brings, in order

Most readers skim in this order. The section order follows it.

1. **Is this the right procedure for me?** What it does, who it suits, and
   what it can't do.
2. **Will it look like I want?** Real results on bodies like hers: similar
   age, size and skin tone, with how long after surgery.
3. **Who would operate on me, and can I check them?** One named surgeon,
   credentials she can verify on the boards' own sites, and what patients
   say.
4. **Is it safe?** The honest risk, sourced, and what this practice does
   about it.
5. **What will it cost, and can I afford it?** Starting price, typical range,
   what's included, what moves the price, financing.
6. **What happens to my life during recovery?** Work, kids, sleeping,
   sitting, driving, pain, when she looks normal in clothes.
7. **What happens if I reach out?** How little she has to commit, who
   contacts her, how fast, and in which language.

## Fears to answer before asking

Name the fear plainly, then answer it with a fact, not a reassurance word.

| Fear                                                   | What answers it on the page                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I'll look fake or overdone"                           | Real results in varied bodies; "natural" shown, not claimed; the surgeon explains how the plan fits her proportions     |
| "Something will go wrong"                              | Sourced complication figures, the specific safety standard (law, technique, one surgeon), what to watch for in recovery |
| "The cheap offer is a trap, and this one might be too" | Starting price, typical range, what's included, and why some offers cost less — without naming competitors              |
| "I'll be upsold or pressured"                          | Free consultation, "your surgeon may tell you it isn't right for you", no countdowns                                    |
| "The surgeon won't be the one operating"               | "One surgeon performs every …", named, with portrait                                                                    |
| "I can't take that much time off, or care for my kids" | Recovery by milestone with sourced days and weeks, and how the practice supports follow-up                              |
| "It'll hurt"                                           | What pain is typical and for how long, sourced; pain control discussed at consultation                                  |
| "People will judge me"                                 | Privacy in tone and imagery: editorial, not glamour; no shame language                                                  |
| "I live out of state"                                  | Virtual consultation first, dates in writing, how many nights to stay; no travel packages                               |
| "It's selfish to spend this on me"                     | Speak to her as a capable adult making an informed choice; never guilt, never "you deserve it" pressure                 |

## What she wants to feel

Confident in her own body again, in control of the decision, and certain she
picked a careful surgeon. The page should feel calm, precise and well lit, like
a well-run clinic rather than a nightclub flyer.

## Persuasion, used honestly

Each principle, and how it shows up on a procedure page:

- **Answer first.** Every question H2 opens with the complete answer in 40–60
  words. It respects her time, and AI engines lift the passage whole.
- **Specific beats superlative.** "10–14 days before most people return to a
  desk job (The Aesthetic Society)" persuades; "fast recovery" doesn't. Every
  number is sourced and attributed exactly.
- **Similar others.** Reviews that name this procedure, photos of bodies like
  hers. If none exist, say less rather than borrow other procedures' praise.
- **Verifiable authority.** One named surgeon, with each credential linked to
  the issuing body's own record. A link she can click beats a badge.
- **Commitment ladder.** Read → see results → two-field form → full form or
  call. Every step is small, and reversible: "free", "no obligation",
  "a coordinator contacts you".
- **CTAs at peaks of intent.** Right after results, after the safety
  checklist, after the price, beside the surgeon, and always on the mobile
  sticky bar. Never before she has had a reason to trust.
- **Loss framing, ethically.** Explain what cheaper offers can leave out
  (anesthesia, facility, the safety standard) so she can compare. Never scare
  her with deaths to sell a consultation. Risk information is for her
  decision, not for urgency.
- **Autonomy.** "Including when this procedure isn't the right choice" builds
  more trust than any claim. Offer the checklist of questions to ask _any_
  surgeon.
- **Cognitive ease.** Short sentences, one idea per paragraph, tables for
  facts, numbered steps for processes, plain words for anatomy ("the layer
  under the skin"), with the technical term once.
- **Language and belonging.** "Hablamos español" in the hero and the forms,
  `lang="es"` on Spanish text, and Spanish reviews shown as Google's
  translation, marked as one.

## Never

- Fake urgency or scarcity: "only 3 spots left", countdowns, "this week only".
- Body-negative words: "flaws", "problem areas", "fix your body", "mommy
  pooch" used as a joke.
- Sexualized imagery or framing: swimwear, lingerie, crops on body parts.
- Testimonials that aren't real reviews, edited reviews, or reviews moved
  under a heading they don't support.
- Guarantees of results, "best", "safest", "painless", "scarless".
- Before/after photos of models, or AI images near results without a label.

## Per-procedure starting hypotheses

Verify each against queries, reviews and sources before using it. Anything
medical needs a source in the facts file; anything about the practice needs
the owner.

- **Tummy tuck.** After pregnancy or weight loss: loose skin and separated
  abdominal muscles that exercise doesn't fix. Fears: the scar (where it sits,
  under underwear or swimwear), time unable to lift her children, drains. She
  compares mini vs full vs with liposuction. Cost queries are large for this
  procedure. Insurance comes up; cosmetic abdominoplasty generally isn't
  covered (source the statement).
- **Mommy makeover.** A mother done having children, combining procedures.
  Fears: time away from her kids, the safety of a long combined surgery,
  hidden costs in a bundle. Wants one plan, one price and one recovery.
  Permission matters: she's often the last person she spends on.
- **Breast augmentation.** Size anxiety ("too big, too fake"), implant safety
  (the FDA's boxed warning and patient checklist, BIA-ALCL, breast implant
  illness), implant lifespan and future surgery, mammograms, breastfeeding.
  Wants to see sizes on bodies like hers and to be guided, not sold up.
- **Breast lift.** Sagging after pregnancy, nursing or weight loss. Lift
  alone or with implants; scar patterns; nipple sensation; future
  breastfeeding. Wants her shape back rather than a bigger size.
- **Breast reduction.** Often physical before cosmetic: back, neck and
  shoulder pain, bra grooves, rashes, exercise limits. Relief is the promise.
  Insurance is a real question (the practice's policy is an owner answer).
  Scars and breastfeeding come up.
- **Liposuction.** Stubborn fat despite diet and exercise, not weight loss.
  Candidacy (near a stable weight, skin quality), which areas, lipo 360,
  swelling and when results show. Florida limits office liposuction volume
  (verify the rule's current text before citing it).
- **Facelift.** Skews older than the core audience. Fear of looking "pulled"
  or "done", visible scars, social downtime (bruising, when she can be seen).
  Wants to look rested and like herself. Neck lift and fat grafting are often
  combined.
- **Blepharoplasty.** Tired or hooded eyes, sometimes a narrowed field of
  vision (that can make it functional and possibly insurable: an owner and
  source question). Short social downtime and hidden scars reassure.
  Upper vs lower lids.
